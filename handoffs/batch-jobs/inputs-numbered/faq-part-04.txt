878: ### Where does a business's information come from?
879: From the evidence. 941: ### Where do the listings come from?
942: From public information: the business's own website, official records such as the federal list of health-care providers (the NPI Registry), open map data, and directories like chambers of commerce. Our crawler gathers what's there, checks that a business is real (its own website, an official record, a government page, two separate sources, or one source whose page says enough about it: that one shows as unconfirmed until more agree), puts it in the category that fits what it says it is, and joins it with what's already listed instead of making a second copy. Each listing goes up unclaimed, ready for its owner to claim. Each fact keeps where it came from, from the first time our crawler sees a business: what it found and which other sources agreed are kept together, and stay with the listing once it goes up (or with the listing it turned out to be). A business we haven't listed yet isn't forgotten either, so the next time it comes up we already know what we've seen. Open map data (OpenStreetMap) only tells our crawler where to look; what we keep is what the business's own site or an official record says.

944: ### How often does your crawler read a website?
945: Only as often as it changes. Our crawler keeps a note of each page it reads (its address, when, and a fingerprint of its text, never a copy of the page) and asks the website first whether the page has changed; a page that hasn't isn't downloaded again. Pages that change often, like an events calendar, are read again within a week or two, pages that don't up to every three months, and never more often than the site's robots.txt allows. It reads a page's own structured data first (schema.org, the same markup search engines read), so a business that puts its name, address, phone and hours there is read just as it wrote them. On a page that lists many places, like a county's services page or a chamber's members, each one is read as its own place, with the kind of business its words describe and its town. Search finds the businesses named on those pages even before they're listed (see below).
946: 
947: ### Can your crawler take a website off a listing by mistake?
948: It shouldn't, and we've made sure a missed visit never counts. Our crawler works in short runs, and a run sometimes ends before it reaches everything it set out to check. Until now, a website it never got to could be counted as one that didn't load, and after two of those it came off the listing. Now a check that wasn't done is simply done later: a website comes off only when our crawler actually tried it twice, at least three days apart, and it didn't load. The same goes for the pages we read: a visit that ends early keeps everything we already knew from that website, and each page replaces only what that same page said before. Guides are protected the same way. If a guide's draft doesn't finish, the guide stays as it was, and one our team took down stays down. A section someone on our team saves while Claude is writing is kept as they wrote it. The monthly load of natural landmarks never deletes a place that's held for a person or a tribal nation to decide on. We're now checking the websites taken off before this change and putting back those that load (coming). If you see a listing missing its website, tap **Suggest an edit** on the listing and tell us.

950: ### How does your crawler choose which websites to read?
951: On its own, for most of them. It meets websites through the pages it reads: when a county page or a chamber's list links to another site, that site joins its list too (a link from a business's name on the page is taken as that business's own website). On each site, it reads the site's map of its pages (its sitemap) and its new pages first, so over a few visits it covers the whole site, not just its front pages. Once a month it also reads the free public web archive Common Crawl, which captures billions of pages: from it, it finds every town, county and state office site in the states we cover, and reads up to 50 pages of each website we read regularly without visiting the site itself. Since 2026-11-20 it finds those pages in Common Crawl's URL Index (its index files for bulk reading, as Common Crawl asks), one request at a time, never by querying its index server in bulk; that server is asked only for a single page's copy when a site doesn't load, at most 12 a run. Every website it meets goes on its list with what kind of site it is, from its address and from what the site says about itself (its title and description). A site that said nothing the first time is asked again two weeks later, and any site still waiting for a decision is looked at again every two months. A **Visit website** link next to a business on a page we read fills in that business's website, rather than being treated as a site to read. When our team reads a site through Listing Intake, it counts as a visit too: its pages go in the crawler's notes, the site joins its list, and the places it names are kept for search where the site's terms allow (never from directories we only cite, or a site our team set aside). Public bodies, like government offices, counties, cities and schools, are read regularly from the start, since what they publish is public. Commercial directories and social media are never copied, only cited. Chambers of commerce, tourism sites, organizations and other sites are classified by the crawler itself from their address and what their home page says (coming: it runs once our indexer is switched on), and read regularly; what they say is kept only as far as their terms and licence allow. Nothing waits on a person any more, except tribal nations' websites, which are always a person's decision. Our team can change any call, and theirs always stands. It reads PDFs as well as web pages, like a county's list of transfer stations or a poster of hours. A PDF that's a picture of a page (a scanned flyer) is read by a text scanner (AWS Textract), and a page that only shows its text in a full browser is read through a service that renders it (Tavily), each within a small monthly budget. Neither is ever used on a page a website asks crawlers not to read.

953: ### Does your crawler keep every link it finds?
954: Yes, as a note, not as a copy (coming: our indexer is built and switched off until we start it). Every link on every page our crawler reads goes into its index, on the same website or another, with the page it came from and the words of the link, so nothing it has seen is lost; there's no longer a limit of 60 links a page or 40 other websites a visit. Keeping a link isn't reading it. A page is read only when its website may be read, its robots.txt allows it, and at a polite pace (one page every second and a half at least, or slower if the site asks). Facebook and Instagram pages are never read, sign-in and account pages never, and a tribal nation's website only when a person on our team says so. A website that blocks us is never tried again by itself; our team decides. A link to a website about somewhere outside the states we cover (another state's own site, like a Kansas city's, or another country's) is kept in the index as **Outside our region** and never read; if our team welcomes that website, it's read like any other (2026-11-19). Pages near the places we already list are read first, then official and first-party websites, then the rest, so the index grows from what matters to people here. What we learn from a page comes back to the index: a page that gave us real places ranks its links higher, and junk (a parked domain, an endless calendar, a search-results page) is remembered as junk by a short fingerprint, so it's never read twice. The indexer works all the time in small batches, so our website never feels it.

982: ### How do you know a listing is right?
983: Our crawler checks each business against public sources: its own website, official records, government pages and directories. Open map data (OpenStreetMap) only tells it where to look: its phone, hours and address are never copied, only what the business's own website or another source we read says. A business found on just one website can be listed when that page gives enough (its street address and phone, say); it's marked unconfirmed and is checked again and again until more sources agree. A town someone searches for is looked at again a month later, and for the kind of place they searched (storage, auto repair, pharmacies) when that isn't one we already look for there. When its own website lists its locations, that wins for each location's phone and hours. When a source shows the business at another address, it isn't listed at the old one until that's sorted out. Each listing shows when it was listed and when it was last checked. Our crawler reads each listing's own website again every month while we're less sure of it (under 51) and every three months once we're surer, and Google is checked at least every three months: it fills in what's missing (never changing what a person wrote), and checks that the business is still there. When the website now shows a different phone number or different hours from the listing, our team gets the new ones to check (a site's number can be a head office's, and hours can be one season's), and the listing changes once they confirm. It also checks the listings against each other for one business listed twice (see "How do you keep a business from being listed twice?"); two businesses sharing one number are left as two. A website that stops working, twice a few days apart, comes off the listing (or goes to our team, if the owner entered it), and it stays off until the business's site works again or someone puts it back. Every change to a listing, by our crawler, our team, the owner or a visitor's accepted fix, is kept with who or what made it, and what it replaced is kept too, so nothing we learned is lost and an old value never quietly comes back. A listing's phone, address or website is changed by what other sources say only when two independent sources agree (one website counts once), and a business's name only ever gets spelled out in full, never swapped for another name. When two listings turn out to be one business, what we knew about both is kept together on the one that stays. When a business's own website says it has closed for good, its listing shows **Possibly closed**, quoting what the site says, with **Still open? Tell us**, and our team checks. It shows Closed (still findable, with **Reopened? Tell us**) only once a person or the owner confirms it: one website alone never closes a listing. If the site stops saying so, the notice goes. When its website says it has moved, our team gets the new address to check (never changed on its own, since one branch moving isn't the business moving). When its website doesn't load for us, we try again a few days later and look for Common Crawl's latest copy of it (a free public archive of the web): if that copy is recent, the site is probably up and refusing our crawler, so our team checks it instead of the website coming off the listing, and a missing description can come from the copy, marked with the date it was copied. A missing phone is looked for in our own copies of the public registries first (the NPI Registry for health care, Recreation.gov for recreation areas), matched by name and street, and only then on the web; a listing our copies or its website flag as possibly closed is first in line for the Google check. If our main mail server doesn't answer, the site's own emails (sign-in codes, notices, receipts) go through our backup mail service, Amazon SES, from send.traversence.com. When the weather service can't give us a forecast, a place's guide shows a link to the National Weather Service's own forecast for it instead. If the check for a place's events fails, it's tried again the next day; otherwise each place is checked weekly. Something wrong? Use **Closed, moved or listed twice? Tell us** on the listing, or claim it if it's yours.

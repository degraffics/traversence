# ADR 0011: AI-Corpus Data Minimization — Context-In, Abstraction-Out

**Status:** Accepted

## Context

The AI topic-extraction pipeline (`architecture.md` §25/§26) reads from a stored corpus that can, going forward, be fed by more than scraped public/partner data — Communities & Groups (§27) can stage thread content into the same corpus to contribute to context-relevancy and topic-relevance scoring. That raises a real risk that didn't exist while the corpus was scraped-public-data-only: a gated, sensitive community conversation could end up either directly quoted in a public article, or reconstructable/fingerprintable from an over-detailed summary field, even with the best intentions of the pipeline built around it. A policy promise ("we won't use it that way") isn't a structural guarantee. This decision is about making the guarantee structural: controlling what actually gets *written* into the corpus at intake, not just how it's used afterward.

## Decision

**Classify at intake; store only the classification.** Incoming content — from any source: community threads (§27), guest submissions (§7, §11, `decisions/0009`), scraped external content (§25), or reviews — is read in full to make a good classification judgment, but only the resulting abstracted category is written to the corpus. The category set: relevancy, topic, context, summary, listing-specific, experience, recommendation, and audience/impact. Raw verbatim content is never persisted into the shared AI corpus, regardless of source.

**Summary must generalize, not paraphrase.** A "summary" field that stays too close to the source text reopens the exact fingerprinting risk that storing raw content would have created — it's a loophole, not a safe field, if it isn't held to a genuine-generalization standard. This is a hard rule on that field, not a style preference: a summary states what kind of thing was said, not a close rewording of what was said.

**Who/What/Where/When/Why + noun/verb keying, with "Who" redefined.** Structured keys — a named local landmark, a specific road, a recurring noun/verb pairing — let the system recognize that something is a legitimate topic worth tracking without storing the private conversational content it came from (e.g., "Old Well Pipes" or "Miller Creek Road" can be noted as topic-scope keys once they're clearly a topic of discussion, without storing the specific conversation that surfaced them). Critically, **"Who" is redefined to mean audience/impact/usefulness** — which persona or audience segment a topic is good for, or what impact/usefulness it has (the existing Resident/Traveler/Community tracks, or a finer segmentation beneath them) — **never** system-user identity. System-user identity stays exclusively governed by `actor_user_id` and profile attribution under `decisions/0010-identity-exposure-and-ai-consent.md`; this framework has no lane for it and must not develop one informally through the "Who" key. Audience/impact classification is itself derived dynamically from the full surrounding conversational context at the moment of classification (context-in) — the global sense of the conversation, not just the isolated instance of the word or notion — while only the resulting abstracted category is what gets stored (abstraction-out). This is the same context-in/abstraction-out principle applied one layer further in than the summary/raw-content split above.

**Topic promotion requires recurrence.** A key is only promoted to legitimate "topic scope" — the kind of thing the AI can target and write about — after it recurs across multiple independent mentions. This is a structural safeguard, not a policy one: a one-off private detail from a single gated conversation cannot become a public trend, because by definition it can't recur if it only ever existed in one place. This is a stronger guarantee than relying on summary quality alone, since it doesn't depend on a classifier getting every summary right.

**Sensitive-source tagging survives into retention/access rules.** Any staged data sourced from a sensitive context (most notably a gated community thread) is tagged by source at staging time, even though only its abstracted classification is stored. This lets retention and access controls target that flagged slice of the corpus specifically, rather than relying on uniform treatment of what is, underneath the abstraction layer, a mixed-sensitivity corpus.

## Consequences

`architecture.md` §26 and the new §27 (Communities & Groups) and §28 (this mechanism, summarized) all point here. The corpus-ingestion layer needs an actual classification step built before anything is persisted — this is a real build requirement on the eventual scraper/staging pipeline, not just a governance note. Retention and access tooling (§14, §24) needs to read and respect the source tag described above. This decision is categorically distinct from `decisions/0010-identity-exposure-and-ai-consent.md`: ADR 0010 governs whether and how *identified user* content is used by AI systems; this ADR governs what gets *stored at all* regardless of identity, and explicitly walls off "audience/impact" from ever becoming a second, informal identity channel. It also bears on `decisions/0004`'s phased embedding-storage work: whatever gets embedded for topic/vector search should be the classified abstraction, not raw source text, which further reduces what a future vector store ever needs to hold.

**Not yet ready for engineering handoff.** This decision specifies the classification *rules*, not the implementation — no table schema for the classified-category store, no classifier/service interface, and no defined mechanism for the frequency/recurrence gate or the source-tagging field exist yet. A developer cannot build the intake pipeline from this ADR alone; it needs a follow-up technical-design pass (schema, service contract, and where in the ingestion flow classification actually runs) before it's assignable.
